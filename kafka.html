Franz Kafka

Writer · Prague · 1883 – 1924

The story

Franz Kafka. Born in Prague in 1883. He studied law. From 1908 he worked in an insurance office, and wrote at night. He also drew. Thin black figures in notebooks. Almost none of it was published. In his lifetime, seven thin books. The rest stayed in drawers.

The first book was Contemplation, in 1912. Eighteen very short pieces, written over eight years. A man at a window. A merchant at the end of his day. Nothing happens, and it is still unsettling. The idea: even an ordinary day, looked at closely, becomes strange.

On the night of the twenty-second of September, 1912, he wrote The Judgment in one sitting. Eight hours. Georg Bendemann writes to a friend abroad that he is engaged. His father sentences him to death by drowning. Georg goes to the bridge. He says: dear parents, I have always loved you. And lets go. Published in 1913. The idea: a father's verdict, accepted without a question.

Two months later, The Metamorphosis. Gregor Samsa, a traveling salesman, wakes one morning as an insect. His first worry is the train. His father throws apples at him. One stays in his back, and rots. Gregor dies. The family takes the tram to the countryside, in the sun. Published in 1915. The idea: a person is valued only while he is useful.

Amerika was begun in 1911. Karl Rossmann is sixteen. He is sent to New York after a servant girl has a child by him. An uncle, a hotel, strangers. He is thrown out of each. Kafka never finished it. It appeared in 1927. The idea: the innocent are passed from one hierarchy to the next.

In July 1914, in a Berlin hotel, he was questioned about his engagement. He called it a tribunal. A few weeks later he began The Trial. Josef K., a bank clerk, is arrested on his thirtieth birthday. No one tells him the charge. A lawyer, a painter, a priest. In the last chapter, two men take him to a quarry. This is a page of the manuscript. He left it unfinished. Published in 1925. The priest tells him a parable. A man waits at the door of the Law all his life. At the end he is told: this door was meant only for you. Now it will be closed. The idea: guilt without a charge, and a court that never explains.

In October 1914 he interrupted The Trial to write In the Penal Colony. A traveler is shown a machine. It writes the sentence on the prisoner's body with needles, over twelve hours. The prisoner is not told his sentence. In the end the officer lies down in the machine himself. Published in 1919. The idea: a law that punishes first, and explains never.

In 1917 he wrote the stories of A Country Doctor. Fourteen of them. In one, an ape named Red Peter reports to a scientific academy. He explains how he became a man. He saw no other way out of the cage. Published in 1919. The idea: what it costs to fit in.

In November 1919 he wrote a letter to his father. About a hundred handwritten pages. He lists what his father said and did. Once, that he would tear him apart like a fish. He gave it to his mother to deliver. It was not delivered. The idea: every later authority has his father's face.

In 1922, already ill, he began The Castle. K., a land surveyor, arrives in a snowbound village. The castle summoned him, and will not receive him. The novel breaks off in the middle of a sentence. Published in 1926. The idea: an authority you cannot reach, and a man who still wants to be admitted.

In 1922 he also wrote A Hunger Artist. A man fasts in a cage at a circus. Forty days, says his manager. After that, the crowds stop coming. In the end a panther is put in his cage. He says he fasted because he never found food he liked. Published in 1924. The idea: art, and an audience that admires but does not understand.

In 1923 he moved to Berlin, with Dora Diamant. Tuberculosis had reached his throat. He died on the third of June, 1924, in a sanatorium near Vienna. He was forty. He had asked his friend Max Brod to burn the rest. Brod read it instead. The Trial. The Castle. Amerika. We have them because one person said no. Franz Kafka. Eighteen eighty-three, to nineteen twenty-four. You understand.
